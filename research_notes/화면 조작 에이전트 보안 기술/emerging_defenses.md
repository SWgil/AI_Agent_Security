# Emerging and Future Security Technologies for Screen/GUI-Operating Agents (state as of 2026-11-18)

- Verification tags: **(fetched)** = I read the primary page in this session; **(search summary)** = taken from a search-result digest of the page, not the full text, so numbers should be re-checked before publication; **(prior knowledge)** = well-known source I did not re-open in this session.

## 1. Architectural defenses with structural/provable guarantees, and whether they survive when the observation is a screenshot and the action is a tap

### Takeaway
Structural defenses (dual-LLM, CaMeL, IFC/taint tracking, policy monitors) are the only family with a credible claim to survive adaptive attackers, but they were designed for structured tool-calling; the one serious port to computer use (single-shot planning, Jan-Mar 2026) guarantees only control-flow integrity, loses roughly a third to a half of task performance, is browser/desktop-only, and is still beaten by "branch steering" through manipulated pixels. No equivalent has been demonstrated for mobile, and nothing in this family is shipped in a consumer screen-operating agent.

### Cited Findings

**Foundational patterns**
- Dual-LLM pattern (privileged LLM never sees untrusted content; quarantined LLM sees it but has no tools) was proposed by Simon Willison, 25 Apr 2023. **[RESEARCH/pattern]** (prior knowledge) — [Willison, "The Dual LLM pattern"](https://simonwillison.net/2023/Apr/25/dual-llm-pattern/)
- CaMeL (Debenedetti, Shumailov, Tramèr, Carlini et al., Google DeepMind/ETH, Mar 2025) extracts control and data flow from the trusted user query into a program, runs it in a custom interpreter, and attaches capabilities to data so policies can block unauthorized flows. **[RESEARCH]** (prior knowledge for design) — [arXiv:2503.18813](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18813). Reported cost: 77% task success under attack vs 84% undefended on AgentDojo with o3, as restated in a 2026 systematization (fetched) — [arXiv:2606.26479](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.26479v1)
- "Design Patterns for Securing LLM Agents against Prompt Injections" (Beurer-Kellner, Tramèr et al., arXiv Jun 2025) describes six patterns (incl. action-selector, plan-then-execute, map-reduce, dual-LLM, code-then-execute, context-minimization) that isolate untrusted data from control flow; its central principle is that once an agent has ingested untrusted input it must be constrained so that input cannot trigger consequential actions; the authors state the patterns trade capability for safety and that general-purpose agents are hard to protect this way, so application-specific designs are the practical route. **[RESEARCH/guidance]** (search summary) — [arXiv:2506.08837](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.08837v3)
- Meta "Agents Rule of Two" (31 Oct 2025): until robustness research can reliably detect and refuse injection, an agent should hold at most two of [A] processing untrustworthy inputs, [B] access to sensitive systems/private data, [C] changing state or communicating externally within a session; needing all three means human supervision. Meta calls prompt injection "a fundamental, unsolved weakness in all LLMs" and says the rule is not "a finish line". **[Guidance; adopted internally at Meta — extent unverified]** — [Meta AI blog](https://ai.meta.com/blog/practical-ai-agent-security/) (search summary); details and critique in [Willison, 2 Nov 2025](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Nov/2/new-prompt-injection-papers/) (fetched)
- Willison's assessment: he "like[s] this a lot" and considers it the best practical guidance available in the absence of dependable defenses; he objected to Meta's diagram labelling the A+C combination "safe" (harm is still possible), and Meta relabelled intersections to "lower risk". (fetched) — [Willison, 2 Nov 2025](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Nov/2/new-prompt-injection-papers/)
- The Rule of Two builds on Willison's "lethal trifecta" (private data + untrusted content + external communication), Jun 2025. (prior knowledge) — [Willison, "The lethal trifecta"](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Jun/16/the-lethal-trifecta/)
- Criticism: the Rule of Two is per-agent/per-session and does not by itself stop a compromise from rippling through interconnected multi-agent systems (secondary commentary, Ken Huang). — [Substack](https://kenhuangus.substack.com/p/the-rule-of-two-vs-reality-why-metas)

**Information-flow control and taint tracking**
- FIDES (Costa et al., Microsoft, arXiv May 2025): a planner that tracks confidentiality and integrity labels, deterministically enforces policies, and adds primitives to selectively hide/reveal information; with policies enabled it stopped all AgentDojo injection attacks that attempted unauthorized actions or data flows. **[RESEARCH -> PILOT]** (search summary) — [arXiv:2505.23643](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.23643v2)
- FIDES is landing as experimental IFC middleware in Microsoft Agent Framework (`agent_framework.security`, reported as of agent-framework-core v1.3.0). **[PILOT, experimental]** (search summary) — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/agent-framework/agents/security); [GitHub discussion #5624](https://github.com/microsoft/agent-framework/discussions/5624)
- RTBAS (arXiv Feb 2025): taint-tracking IFC for tool-based agents using two "dependency screeners" (LM-as-judge and attention-based saliency) to decide when untrusted data influenced a tool call — i.e. the propagation step itself is probabilistic, unlike CaMeL/FIDES. **[RESEARCH]** (search summary) — [arXiv:2502.08966](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08966)

**Policy / guardrail languages (reference monitors)**
- Progent (arXiv Apr 2025): a reference monitor between agent and tools enforcing fine-grained privilege policies (which tools, which arguments, under which conditions); original paper reports AgentDojo ASR 39.9% -> 1.0%. **[RESEARCH]** (search summary; number restated in fetched 2606.26479) — [arXiv:2504.11703](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.11703)
- AgentSpec (Wang, Poskitt, Sun; arXiv Mar 2025): a DSL of trigger/predicate/enforcement rules for runtime constraint of agents. **[RESEARCH]** (prior knowledge; my search in this session returned nothing on it — verify the ID) — [arXiv:2503.18666](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18666)
- An independent (non-peer-reviewed, small-scale) June 2026 reproduction systematized Dual-LLM, CaMeL, FIDES, Progent, Conseca, RTBAS and FORGE as "out-of-band" defenses and noted that "every one of them is validated only on static benchmarks". Its own hand-written defense-aware attack on Progent did not raise ASR (mean 4.2% standard vs 2.6% adaptive, undefended 25.8%), but utility under attack fell from 45.2% to ~26-27% and defended runs needed ~15x more LLM calls; authors call it "a preliminary data point, not a proof" (7B model, 8 tasks/suite, one attack template). (fetched) — [arXiv:2606.26479](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.26479v1)

**Adaptation to computer-use agents**
- "CaMeLs Can Use Computers Too" (Foerster, Blanchard, Nikolić, Shumailov, Zhang, Mullins, Papernot, Tramèr, Zhao; arXiv 2601.09923, v2 dated 6 Mar 2026) is the main attempt to port the dual-LLM/CaMeL idea to CUAs. **[RESEARCH]** (fetched) — [arXiv:2601.09923v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.09923v2)
  - Design: *Single-Shot Planning* — a privileged planner (P-LLM) emits "a complete execution graph with conditional branches" before seeing any screen; a quarantined vision model (Q-VLM) handles screenshots/DOM through narrow tools (`summarize_screenshot`, `find`, `find_element_by_text`, `verify_hypothesis`, `check_done`) in an Observe-Verify-Act loop.
  - Guarantee claimed: "provable control flow integrity guarantees against arbitrary instruction injections". Not claimed: data-flow security — "the data flow remains vulnerable"; "a compromised Q-VLM could return false coordinates or summaries to steer the plan into malicious branches".
  - New attack class it defines: *Branch Steering* — manipulate what is on screen (fake cookie banner inside an ad; gradient-optimized pixels) so a legitimate branch of the pre-approved plan does the attacker's bidding. The pixel attack "evades both screenshot-based and DOM-enhanced Q-VLM verification".
  - Mitigations tested (DOM consistency checks, multi-modal consensus between a DOM-reading model and a screenshot-reading model) "provide empirical defense-in-depth but do not offer formal guarantees beyond the core Dual-LLM control flow protection"; HTML5 ads bypass the DOM check; multi-modal consensus raises token use up to x6.57.
  - Utility: abstract claims retention of "up to 57%" of frontier-model performance and up to +19% for small open-source models on OSWorld; e.g. UI-TARS-1.5-7B 24.4% alone vs 29.0% with a GPT-5 planner on all tasks. The body also uses pass@5 figures (65-68% of each CUA's previously solved tasks) which assume oracle selection — different bases, do not mix. Token overhead x1.88 for the CaMeL-style setup, x29.60 for a FIDES-style variant.
  - Stated limits: browser-based tasks only; underspecified tasks force many branches; semantic security policies for CUA tools "remain an open question"; no mobile/Android evaluation.
  - Deployment idea: proprietary model plans, an open-source model does perception locally (which also keeps screenshots on-device).
- For mobile specifically, the August 2026 Radboud paper "Not an A11y" recommends (but does not evaluate) agent-side provenance tracking "for every observed UI element, including its source application", data/instruction separation, treating notifications/webpages/hidden nodes as data, policy checks on unexpected cross-app actions (e.g. opening Settings while summarizing), and consistency checks between accessibility tree and screenshot; it warns that vision "should be viewed as supplementary context rather than a reliable security boundary" and that "prompt-level delimiters may provide limited protection". **[RESEARCH, recommendations only]** (fetched) — [arXiv:2608.08939](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.08939v1)
  - Evidence base in the same paper: open-source Android agents (MobileRun v0.6.0, Mobile-Use v3.3.0) with Gemma/Qwen backends were hijacked via notifications, overlays, hidden accessibility metadata, calendar/notes/contact fields, with aggregate ASR between 0.15 and 0.82 by framework/model; poisoned planner "AppCards" reached ASR 1.0. Open-source frameworks, not vendor products.
- Further 2026 mobile attack/benchmark work that defines what a defense must handle (titles only verified): MIRAGE (injection via user-generated content regions, May 2026) [arXiv:2605.28116](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.28116); action-rebinding attacks on Android GUI agents [arXiv:2601.12349](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.12349); UI desynchronization ("When Agents See Differently") [arXiv:2609.16732](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.16732); MobileWorldSafety [arXiv:2608.17659](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.17659); DIBench [arXiv:2610.06898](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.06898); OS-Sentinel hybrid validation for mobile agents (Oct 2025) [arXiv:2510.24411](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.24411). (search summary; contents not read)

### Inferences
- Why GUI agents are structurally harder: CaMeL/FIDES/Progent rely on (a) a typed action space where arguments carry meaning ("send_email(to=...)") and (b) data with identifiable provenance. A tap at (x, y) has no semantics until the screen is interpreted, and a screenshot is a single blob mixing trusted chrome, the user's own data, and attacker-controlled content. So labels cannot be attached at the granularity IFC needs, and "policy on arguments" degenerates to policy on coordinates.
- Consequently the realistic adaptations are: (1) plan before observing (single-shot planning) and accept reduced adaptivity; (2) lift the action space from pixels to semantic actions (accessibility node + owning app/package, or structured app functions — see section 7) so that reference monitors and taint labels have something to bind to; (3) confine with Rule-of-Two-style capability removal per task rather than try to label pixels.
- Mobile gives the OS an advantage the web lacks: Android/iOS know which package drew each window and each accessibility node, so per-element provenance (a prerequisite for IFC on GUI) is technically available to a first-party agent. I found no paper or product that exposes this as a security label to the model — this is an inference, not a reported design.
- Applying the Rule of Two to a phone agent is uncomfortable: a general phone agent almost always has A (any app screen can contain third-party content), B (the phone is the user's most sensitive device) and C (it taps). The rule therefore implies either human-in-the-loop for C or per-task narrowing of A/B (app allowlists), which matches what vendors ship (section 3).
- The maturity ranking for GUI agents is roughly: Rule of Two / design patterns (usable today as design discipline) > policy monitors on semantic actions (feasible, unevaluated on mobile) > single-shot planning (research, browser only) > full IFC on pixels (no known solution).

### Gaps
- No evaluation of CaMeL-style planning, FIDES or Progent on AndroidWorld or any mobile benchmark was found.
- No strong adaptive-attack evaluation (Attacker-Moves-Second scale) of out-of-band defenses exists; the only one found is small and non-peer-reviewed.
- Invariant Labs' guardrail language and Google's Conseca/FORGE were named in sources but not researched in this session.
- Whether any shipped computer-use product internally uses plan-then-execute or privilege separation is not publicly documented in what I found.
- "Set-of-marks / accessibility-tree filtering as a sanitization defense": I found the idea only as recommendations (cross-modal consistency checks); no rigorous paper showing it resists adaptive attackers. The CaMeL-CUA results suggest it does not (pixel attack passes both modalities).

## 2. Model- and detection-level defenses and their limits

### Takeaway
Adversarial training, classifiers, guard models and prompt-level tricks reduce the success of known attacks and are what vendors actually ship, but the 2025-2026 evidence is that every published defense of this kind falls to an attacker who adapts; vendors themselves now say the problem will not be fully solved at this layer.

### Cited Findings
- "The Attacker Moves Second" (Nasr, Carlini, et al.; 14 authors across OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind; arXiv 10 Oct 2025) tested 12 published jailbreak/prompt-injection defenses with adaptive attacks (gradient, RL, search, human red-teaming) and got ASR above 90% for most, though most had reported near-zero. (search summary + fetched Willison digest) — [arXiv:2510.09023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.09023); [Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Nov/2/new-prompt-injection-papers/)
  - Examples: Spotlighting 28% (static) -> 99% (adaptive); RPO 0% -> 99%. Broken defenses include Spotlighting, Prompt Sandwiching, RPO (prompting), and Data Sentinel, MELON (secret-knowledge/canary detection). Human red-teaming (about 500 participants, $20,000 prize pool) defeated all defenses (100%). Gradient methods were the least effective automated attack; search with LLM-generated candidates and RL worked better.
  - Methodological conclusion: evaluating on a fixed set of attack strings is unsound; defenses must be tested against attackers who know the defense.
- Willison does not share the paper's optimism that reliable defenses will arrive soon. (fetched) — [Willison, 2 Nov 2025](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Nov/2/new-prompt-injection-papers/)
- OpenAI (22 Dec 2025, on ChatGPT Atlas) stated prompt injection is "unlikely to ever be fully solved"; it built an RL-trained LLM "automated attacker" to find injections against the browser agent and adversarially trains against its findings; one found exploit made the agent send a resignation letter instead of an out-of-office reply; OpenAI declined to say whether the update measurably reduced successful injections. **[SHIPPED: adversarial training + automated red-teaming loop]** (search summary) — [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/hardening-atlas-against-prompt-injection/); [TechCrunch 22 Dec 2025](https://techcrunch.com/2025/12/22/openai-says-ai-browsers-may-always-be-vulnerable-to-prompt-injection-attacks/)
- Anthropic's browser-agent numbers (Claude for Chrome research preview, Aug 2025): 23.6% attack success without mitigations, 11.2% with safeguards in autonomous mode. **[SHIPPED mitigations, residual risk disclosed]** (prior knowledge; not re-fetched) — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-for-chrome)
- Google's Gemini API computer-use documentation carries a preview warning that the feature may contain errors and security vulnerabilities, and offers opt-in screenshot scanning to detect hidden adversarial instructions. **[PILOT]** (search summary) — [Gemini API docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/computer-use.md.txt)
- Google's May 2026 Android post says new platform protections against prompt injection activate when Gemini acts for the user, likened to those in Chrome's auto browse; no mechanism or measured rate is given. **[SHIPPED/rolling out, undocumented]** (fetched, Polish edition) — [Google blog, 12 May 2026](https://blog.google/intl/pl-pl/nowosci-produktowe/android-chrome-play/przyszlosc-androida-oparta-na-agentach-tworzenie-gemini-intelligence-na-fundamencie-bezpieczenstwa-i-prywatnosci/)
- For GUI agents specifically, detection through a second modality is not robust: gradient-optimized banner pixels evaded both screenshot and DOM-based verification models (fetched) — [arXiv:2601.09923v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.09923v2); and adding vision to an accessibility-tree agent had mixed effects (Gemma mean ASR 0.89 -> 0.62, still high) (fetched) — [arXiv:2608.08939](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.08939v1)
- MIRAGE's appendix probes a lightweight VLM classifier asking whether a screenshot is an authentic unmodified mobile UI; effectiveness not visible in the snippet. **[RESEARCH]** (search summary) — [arXiv:2605.28116](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.28116v1)
- Google DeepMind's own account of defending Gemini (adversarial fine-tuning plus adaptive evaluation) is the standard reference for "model hardening helps but needs defense in depth". (prior knowledge; not re-fetched) — [arXiv:2505.14534](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.14534)

### Inferences
- These defenses are probabilistic: they change the attacker's cost, not the possibility. For a screen agent an injection can be rendered text, an image, an overlay, a notification, or hidden accessibility metadata, so the input space a classifier must cover is larger than for text tools, and pixel-level adversarial examples add a channel that text classifiers never see.
- The realistic role of this layer in a future architecture is noise reduction (stop commodity attacks, cut HITL prompts) under a structural layer that bounds damage when it fails. Single-digit residual ASR is unacceptable when the action is a payment or data deletion, since attackers retry at scale.
- Vendor ASR figures are measured against the vendor's own attack set; per "Attacker Moves Second" they should be read as upper bounds on robustness, not guarantees.

### Gaps
- No published adaptive-attack evaluation of any shipped mobile agent's injection defenses (Gemini on Android, Apple, Samsung) was found.
- Post-2025 Anthropic and Google attack-success figures for browser/computer-use were not retrieved in this session (the search returned none); the Anthropic number above is from memory of the Aug 2025 post.
- OpenAI has not published before/after rates for its Atlas hardening (per TechCrunch).
- I did not find 2026 follow-ups to "The Attacker Moves Second" by the same group; whether newer training-based defenses (e.g. instruction-hierarchy training, SecAlign-style) survive comparable attacks is unverified here.

## 3. OS/platform-level models proposed for agents

### Takeaway
Platform vendors are converging on a recognizable set of controls — per-app allowlists for the agent, mandatory confirmation for purchases, a non-dismissable "agent is acting" indicator, isolated execution for automation, an activity log, and confidential-compute backends — and Google began shipping a version of this on Android in 2026. The more ambitious ideas (task-scoped intent permissions, agent-unforgeable trusted confirmation with action binding, selective on-device redaction of screenshots) are still research.

### Cited Findings

**Trusted confirmation paths the agent cannot spoof**
- Existing primitive: Android Protected Confirmation renders a prompt from Trusted UI in the TEE and returns a signed statement that the user confirmed that exact message; designed for high-assurance transactions, not for agents, and available only on supporting hardware. **[SHIPPED primitive, not wired to agents]** (prior knowledge; not re-fetched) — [Android developers](https://developer.android.com/privacy-and-security/security-android-protected-confirmation)
- "Approve What, Exactly? It distinguishes "in-channel" confirmation, where the agent renders the question and can write to the answer channel. **[RESEARCH]** (search summary; modeled approver, not a user study) — [arXiv:2610.21063](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.21063)
- "The Verifiable Action Card" (arXiv Sept 2026, browser agents): the confirmation shown to the user should be derived from the resolved action and rendered on a trusted surface, not generated by the model; only structurally detected commits are confirmed and the prominent card is reserved for elevated-risk actions. **[RESEARCH]** (search summary) — [arXiv:2609.18411](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.18411)

### Inferences
- The app allowlist + purchase confirmation + isolated window combination is a practical Rule-of-Two implementation: the allowlist narrows B, the virtual window narrows A (no notifications/overlays from other apps in view), confirmation gates C. This is the most important "future" pattern already crossing into products.
- A per-task virtual display plausibly removes several attack vectors measured in "Not an A11y" (cross-app overlays, notifications) but not in-app content (webviews, user-generated content, hidden accessibility nodes inside the target app). I found no measurement of this.
- Trusted confirmation is necessary but not sufficient: the research shows the hard part is *binding* (what the user sees equals what will execute) and *volume* (flooding/fatigue), not the hardware root of trust. A TEE prompt that displays agent-authored text is still spoofable in meaning. Mobile is well placed here because the OS owns a surface apps and agents cannot draw over.
- Redaction and injection defense interact: on-device element segmentation used for privacy masking is the same machinery needed for provenance labels and observation filtering; a single on-device "screen firewall" serving both purposes is a plausible direction (inference; not proposed in the sources read).
- "Agent-specific identities/accounts" at OS level (a separate Android user/profile or uid for the agent, with its own permission grants) is a natural extension of the allowlist, but I found no primary source describing it as shipped.

### Gaps
- No primary technical documentation of Android's virtual-display/sandbox mechanism for Gemini automation was found; the "Android Halo/AISeal" claims are unverified.
- Apple's and Samsung's 2026 platform models for screen-operating agents were not researched in this session.
- No source showing Android Protected Confirmation (or any TEE-rendered prompt) actually used for agent action confirmation in a product.
- "Intent-based / task-scoped permissions" as an OS feature: found only as vendor per-app settings and as research policies (Progent, Conseca); no OS permission model that scopes grants to a stated task was found.
- No user studies on whether selective redaction preserves agent task success at scale; results are from small or synthetic benchmarks.

## 4. Agent identity, authorization and delegation

### Takeaway
Identity and delegation is the most standards-active area: IETF now has a Web Bot Auth working-group draft, several OAuth/agent-auth drafts, and the payment networks have competing-but-layerable mandate/token schemes. Almost all of it assumes the agent reaches services through HTTP APIs with its own credentials — which is precisely what an on-device GUI agent tapping through the user's logged-in app does not do, so these mechanisms mostly help by giving agents a reason to leave the GUI for a structured channel at the moment of authorization or payment.

### Cited Findings

**Recognizing agents on the web**
- IETF Web Bot Auth: `draft-ietf-webbotauth-httpsig-protocol` (WG draft, -00 Sept 2026, -01 Oct 2026) lets automated agents sign HTTP requests with HTTP Message Signatures; a `Signature-Agent` header points to an HTTPS URL publishing the keys; motivated by spoofable User-Agent strings and unscalable IP allowlists/shared secrets. **[PILOT: standards-track draft; earlier individual drafts by Meunier (Cloudflare)]** (search summary) — [datatracker](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/draft-ietf-webbotauth-httpsig-protocol); [architecture draft, Mar 2026](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/draft-meunier-web-bot-auth-architecture)
- The drafts state explicitly that a valid signature does not establish who operates the agent, whether it is benign, or whether it is authorized — those remain origin policy — and it does not stop an agent abandoning one identity for another. (search summary) — [draft-ietf-webbotauth-httpsig-protocol-00](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/draft-ietf-webbotauth-httpsig-protocol-00)

**OAuth / identity for agents**
- `draft-klrc-aiagent-auth-03` (July 2026, Informational; authors from AWS, Zscaler, Ping Identity, OpenAI, Okta): best practices for agent authentication/authorization built on WIMSE (workload identity; SPIFFE-style identifiers) and OAuth 2.0; requires agents to be uniquely identified for authentication, authorization, auditing and delegation, with the WIMSE identifier as primary agent identifier. **[PILOT: individual draft]** (search summary) — [datatracker](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/draft-klrc-aiagent-auth-03)
- "OAuth 2.0 Extension: On-Behalf-Of User Authorization for AI Agents" adds parameters to the authorization-code flow so the agent's identity is carried through consent into the access token; revision -02 (Aug 2025) had not been adopted by the OAuth WG at the time of the cited article. **[RESEARCH/draft]** (search summary, secondary) — [WorkOS explainer](https://workos.com/blog/oauth-on-behalf-of-ai-agents)
- `draft-sharif-openid-agent-identity` (-00 Mar 2026, -01 Aug 2026): OpenID Connect claims for autonomous agents (identity, ownership, trust posture, authorized capabilities). **[draft, individual]** (search summary) — [datatracker](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-sharif-openid-agent-identity/)
- The OpenID Foundation published a whitepaper on identity management for agentic AI covering current resources and a strategic agenda for agent authentication, authorization and identity. **[guidance]** (search summary; date not confirmed in results — I recall Oct 2025) — [mirror](https://aitopics.org/doc/arxivorg:84F023AD)
- MCP authorization: a May 2026 CSA note says the spec defines an OAuth 2.1 framework but marks authorization optional; a vendor blog says the 2026-07-28 revision requires OAuth 2.1 for HTTP transports, resource indicators (RFC 8707) and audience validation. **[SHIPPED spec; requirement level conflicting between sources — check the official changelog]** (search summary) — [CSA note](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-mcp-security-crisis-20260504/); [WorkOS](https://workos.com/blog/mcp-supply-chain-security)

**Payments: verifiable intent and scoped credentials**
- Google AP2 (announced Sept 2025): open protocol using signed "mandates" built on verifiable credentials — an Intent Mandate capturing the conditions under which the agent may buy (for human-not-present), a Cart Mandate as the user's explicit authorization of an exact cart and price (human-present), and a Payment Mandate signalling agent involvement and user presence to network/issuer. **[PILOT: open spec, partner ecosystem; production volume unknown]** (search summary) — [ap2-protocol.org](https://ap2-protocol.org/); [core concepts](https://ap2-protocol.org/topics/core-concepts); [Google Cloud announcement](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/announcing-agents-to-payments-ap2-protocol/?hl=en)
  - Sources conflict on details: one explainer describes "Checkout Mandate + Payment Mandate" naming; sources differ on which mandate is signed in the human-not-present flow and on spec version (a "v0.2" defining human-not-present; a "February 2026" release). Unresolved — check the spec repo.
- OpenAI/Stripe Agentic Commerce Protocol (ACP), Apache-2.0, 29 Sept 2025: agent-to-merchant checkout; Stripe's Shared Payment Token is a limited reference to a stored payment method with usage limits and expiry that never reveals the PAN; a secondary source says each token is scoped to one seller, currency, maximum amount and expiry. **[SHIPPED (ChatGPT Instant Checkout) / expanding]** (search summary) — [Stripe docs](https://docs.stripe.com/agentic-commerce/concepts/shared-payment-tokens)
- Mastercard Agent Pay (announced 29 Apr 2025): registered/verified agents transact using "Agentic Tokens" extending MDES tokenization. **[PILOT -> SHIPPED; "US rollout complete, global mid-2026" is a single secondary claim]** (search summary, secondary) — [eco.com comparison](https://eco.com/support/en/articles/15192003-mastercard-agent-pay-vs-visa-trusted-agent-2026-compared)
- Visa Trusted Agent Protocol (unveiled 14 Oct 2025): helps merchants recognize trusted agents and separate them from malicious bots; built on signed HTTP requests (Web Bot Auth lineage); secondary sources disagree whether it only identifies agents or also authorizes payments. **[PILOT]** (search summary, secondary) — [eco.com comparison](https://eco.com/support/en/articles/15192003-mastercard-agent-pay-vs-visa-trusted-agent-2026-compared)
- Research critiques of approval flows in these protocols (label spoofing, forged narration, flooding) — see section 3. — [arXiv:2610.21063](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.21063)

### Inferences
- A GUI agent that types the user's saved card into a web checkout or taps "Pay" in a logged-in app uses none of this: the merchant sees the user's session, the issuer sees a normal card-present-on-file transaction, and there is no signed mandate, no agent identity, no spend cap. The standards therefore create an *incentive gradient*: the high-risk final step (auth, payment) moves to a structured, cryptographically attributable channel even if browsing remained visual. This hybrid is the likely end state for payments.
- Transaction limits enforced by a party other than the model (token scoped to merchant/amount/expiry; issuer-side agentic token controls) are among the few defenses in this whole report that are indifferent to prompt injection — the injected agent simply cannot exceed the token. This is "bound the blast radius" at the money layer.
- Web Bot Auth is designed for cloud-hosted agents with stable keys. For an on-device agent driving the user's own browser/app, who holds the key (OS vendor? agent vendor? per-device attestation?) is unspecified in what I read; it also raises a privacy trade-off (sites learn that the user is using an agent).
- Agent-specific accounts (the agent logs in as a delegated principal with reduced scope rather than as the user) would need site-side adoption of delegation tokens; GUI agents today inherit the user's full session, which is the root of "ambient authority".

### Gaps
- Primary texts of AP2, Visa TAP and Mastercard Agent Pay were not fetched; statements rest on search digests and secondary explainers, which conflict in places.
- Adoption data (how many merchants/sites verify Web Bot Auth signatures or accept AP2 mandates as of Nov 2026) not found.
- No source found on how on-device mobile agents obtain or attest agent identity keys.
- W3C work (e.g. AI agent protocol community group, verifiable credentials for agents) was not researched.
- OpenID Foundation whitepaper date and specifics were not confirmed in this session.

## 5. Runtime monitoring, auditing and recovery

### Takeaway
Human confirmation is the most widely shipped last line of defense and the research consensus in 2026 is that it degrades quickly (fatigue, flooding, spoofed prompts); the proposed fixes are risk-proportional prompting, action-bound trusted rendering, and designing for recovery — but true undo exists only for a minority of actions, and tamper-evident agent logs are proposals rather than products.

### Cited Findings
- Approval fatigue: secondhand reporting attributes to Anthropic (Mar 2026) that users approve 93% of Claude Code permission prompts. (search summary, secondary; not verified against Anthropic's post) — [changkun.de blog](https://changkun.de/blog/ideas/human-in-the-loop-agents/)
- A simulation study reported by IANS found humans approved about one-third of malicious coding-agent commands; conclusion: approval should not be the primary safeguard. (search summary, secondary) — [IANS](https://www.ians.com/news/approval-fatigue-the-hidden-risk-of-human-oversight-of-ai-agents)
- The 2026 Singapore Consensus document notes that both Perplexity and Anthropic identify approval fatigue as a core vulnerability. (search summary) — [arXiv:2608.14611](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.14611)
- Approval flooding succeeded 0.45-0.60 of the time against a modeled approver even with trusted rendering; trusted card display reduced forged-narration success 0.54 -> 0.03. **[RESEARCH]** (search summary) — [arXiv:2610.21063](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.21063)
- Risk-proportional confirmation: confirm only structurally detected commit actions and reserve the prominent card for elevated-risk actions. **[RESEARCH]** (search summary) — [arXiv:2609.18411](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.18411)
- Approval hijacking ("Loopjacking"): the approved operation differs from the executed one. **[RESEARCH, attack]** (search summary) — [arXiv:2609.21081](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.21081)
- Reversibility for GUI agents: "Task-State Governance: A Virtual Machine for Verifiable and Recoverable GUI Agent Execution" (arXiv Oct 2026) separates reversible, compensatable and irreversible actions and warns against "the dangerous illusion of complete reversibility". **[RESEARCH]** (search summary) — [arXiv:2610.20100](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.20100)
- IBM Research STRATUS: checkpoints plus an undo operator for each agent action ("undo-and-retry"), in a cloud-operations setting. **[RESEARCH]** (search summary) — [IBM Research](https://research.ibm.com/blog/undo-agent-for-cloud)
- "Infrastructure for AI Agents" (Chan et al., Jan 2025) proposes rollback infrastructure among agent infrastructure primitives and notes monitoring/approving rollback requests "could be resource intensive in terms of human or AI labour". **[RESEARCH/position]** (search summary) — [arXiv:2501.10114](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.10114)
- "Parallax" (Apr 2026) argues for separating reasoning from acting and requiring human approval for irreversible external actions as a top-tier policy. **[RESEARCH]** (search summary) — [arXiv:2604.12986](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.12986)
- Shipped transparency on Android: live progress view, non-dismissable indicator, and a planned 24-hour assistant-activity view in the privacy dashboard. **[SHIPPED / announced]** (fetched) — [Google blog, 12 May 2026](https://blog.google/intl/pl-pl/nowosci-produktowe/android-chrome-play/przyszlosc-androida-oparta-na-agentach-tworzenie-gemini-intelligence-na-fundamencie-bezpieczenstwa-i-prywatnosci/)
- Hybrid runtime validation for mobile agents (formal verifier for explicit system-level violations plus a VLM judge for contextual risk): OS-Sentinel, Oct 2025. **[RESEARCH]** (search summary, title/abstract level) — [arXiv:2510.24411](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.24411)
- TEE attestation that a guardrail actually ran ("Proof-of-Guardrail", Mar 2026) and TEE attestations of agent execution for payments. **[RESEARCH]** (search summary, title level) — [arXiv:2603.05786](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.05786v2); [arXiv:2511.15712](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.15712)

### Inferences
- For a phone agent the irreversible set is large and common: sending a message, paying, posting, deleting, changing security settings. Reversibility must be classified per action and the classification must not come from the injected model. With pixel actions this classification is itself hard (which tap is "the commit"?) — another argument for semantic action layers.
- A per-task virtual display/sandbox makes local state snapshotting plausible (roll back app state inside the sandbox), but effects that leave the device (a sent transfer) are at best compensatable. A realistic recovery design combines delayed commit ("undo send" windows), spend caps, and post-hoc dispute evidence from logs.
- Logs become evidence for liability disputes (did the user or the agent authorize this?), which is why tamper evidence and binding to signed mandates (AP2) matter; the OS privacy-dashboard log is a start but, as described, is a user-facing activity view rather than a tamper-evident audit trail.
- Anomaly detection on agent behavior (e.g. "task is summarizing a page but agent opened Settings") is the GUI analogue of control-flow integrity; it is implementable as a policy check on app/package transitions, which the OS can observe reliably even when it cannot understand pixels.

### Gaps
- No real-user study of confirmation fatigue specifically for mobile/GUI agents was found; figures are from coding agents or simulations and partly secondhand.
- No shipped tamper-evident (hash-chained/attested) action log for a consumer screen agent was found.
- Behavioral anomaly detection for GUI agents: no strong primary evaluation located in this session.
- The "93%" figure needs verification against Anthropic's original publication.

## 6. Multi-agent and long-horizon issues

### Takeaway
Persistent memory, planner hints, skills/tools marketplaces and agent-to-agent channels turn a one-shot injection into a durable compromise; 2026 saw real marketplace poisoning at scale and a wave of memory-poisoning papers, while defenses (provenance-tagged memory, write-time screening, signed/scanned skills, mandatory OAuth in MCP) are early and carry utility costs.

### Cited Findings
- Planner-level poisoning on mobile: poisoned MobileRun "AppCards" (per-app guidance injected into planning, not passing through the accessibility tree) achieved ASR 1.0 for both tested models in reasoning configurations. **[RESEARCH, attack]** (fetched) — [arXiv:2608.08939](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.08939v1)
- The same paper recommends that in multi-agent pipelines "content-derived subgoals should remain marked as untrusted" and executors independently verify actions. (fetched) — same source
- OWASP's agentic Top 10 lists memory poisoning as ASI06 with five layers: input moderation, memory sanitization with provenance, trust-aware retrieval, behavioral monitoring, forensics; a reference "Agent Memory Guard" screens memory reads/writes (release date reported inconsistently). **[guidance / early tooling]** (search summary, secondary) — [Kiteworks summary](https://kiteworks.substack.com/p/ai-agent-memory-poisoning-owasp-defense)
- Empirical trade-off: a trust-based memory defense prevented poisoning but eliminated the benefit of long-term memory. **[RESEARCH]** (search summary) — [arXiv:2601.05504](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.05504)
- 2026 memory-poisoning detection/audit/benchmark work: MemAudit (post-hoc causal attribution, May 2026) [arXiv:2605.23723](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.23723); MemSentry (Sept 2026) [arXiv:2609.08747](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.08747); MemForm benchmark (Oct 2026) [arXiv:2610.14431](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.14431). **[RESEARCH]** (titles only)
- Skill supply chain: a CSA research note (24 Jun 2026) reports researchers confirmed over 1,184 malicious skills on ClawHub (OpenClaw's marketplace) between Feb and May 2026 and cites a May 2026 study (arXiv:2605.11418) arguing code scanners cannot protect against skill-marketplace poisoning. **[real-world incidents]** (search summary; underlying study not read) — [CSA](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-ai-skill-supply-chain-attacks-20260624-csa/)
- MCP tool poisoning (hidden instructions in tool descriptions), first shown by Invariant Labs in Apr 2025; CSA's May 2026 note calls MCP's issues systemic design flaws. (search summary) — [CSA](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-mcp-security-crisis-20260504/)
- AppFunctions (Android's structured agent interface) has its own reported risk classes: privacy leakage through tool outputs, isolation bypass through over-broad function exposure, and LLM integrity attacks via malicious tool descriptions. (search summary; from a community GitHub project citing research — secondary) — [appfunction-guard](https://github.com/yangyang2857-rgb/appfunction-guard)
- Rule of Two does not by itself contain cross-agent propagation. (secondary commentary) — [Substack](https://kenhuangus.substack.com/p/the-rule-of-two-vs-reality-why-metas)

### Inferences
- For screen agents the "supply chain" includes things that do not look like code: per-app playbooks/skills, cached UI maps, learned shortcuts, and app-provided function descriptions. Each is text the planner trusts more than screen content, so poisoning them bypasses observation-level defenses entirely (the AppCard result shows this). They need the integrity treatment given to code: signing, provenance, review, least privilege.
- Moving from GUI to structured interfaces does not eliminate injection; it relocates it to tool descriptions and tool outputs (MCP tool poisoning; AppFunctions risk classes). The gain is that structural defenses (section 1) then apply.
- Memory provenance is the long-horizon analogue of taint tracking: a memory written while processing untrusted screen content should carry a low-integrity label and never be promoted to instruction. No evidence of this in shipped phone assistants was found.

### Gaps
- A2A protocol security (agent cards, signing, authentication) was not researched in this session.
- No studies of memory poisoning specifically in shipped mobile assistants (Gemini, Siri, Galaxy AI) were found.
- The 1,184 figure and arXiv:2605.11418 claims come via CSA's note; originals not read.
- Current normative status of OAuth in the MCP spec (optional vs required for HTTP) conflicts between sources.

## 7. Predicted direction and what remains unsolved

### Takeaway
The credible consensus is (a) prompt injection will not be solved inside the model, so security must come from system design that bounds what a compromised agent can do, and (b) agents will move toward structured interfaces where they exist, with GUI operation remaining as a long-tail fallback under tighter confinement. What nobody has is a defense that preserves a general-purpose screen agent's utility while giving guarantees against an adaptive attacker who controls part of the screen.

### Cited Findings
- Vendor acknowledgment: Meta — prompt injection is "a fundamental, unsolved weakness in all LLMs" [Meta, 31 Oct 2025](https://ai.meta.com/blog/practical-ai-agent-security/); OpenAI — "unlikely to ever be fully solved" [OpenAI, Dec 2025](https://openai.com/index/hardening-atlas-against-prompt-injection/). (search summaries)
- Researchers from OpenAI, Anthropic and DeepMind jointly showed existing detection/prompting defenses fail under adaptive attack. — [arXiv:2510.09023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.09023)
- Willison: not optimistic that reliable defenses arrive soon; the Rule of Two is the best available practical guidance. (fetched) — [Willison, 2 Nov 2025](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Nov/2/new-prompt-injection-papers/)
- Design-patterns authors (incl. Tramèr): general-purpose agents are hard to secure; application-specific agents with deliberate capability limits are the practical route, and system-level design can make agents secure even if the model is vulnerable. (search summary) — [arXiv:2506.08837](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.08837v3)
- CaMeL-CUA authors (Shumailov, Tramèr, Papernot et al.): UI workflows are structurally predictable enough for upfront planning; but data-flow attacks (branch steering) and semantic policies for CUA tools remain open. (fetched) — [arXiv:2601.09923v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.09923v2)
- Platform direction on Android is explicitly hybrid: structured AppFunctions where apps adopt them, UI automation as the fallback, both expanding in Android 17. (search summary, secondary) — [FoneArena](https://www.fonearena.com/blog/476208/google-android-roadmap-ai-agents-app-automation.html)
- Payments are moving to structured, signed channels (AP2 mandates, ACP scoped tokens, network agentic tokens) rather than agents typing card numbers into forms. (search summary) — [ap2-protocol.org](https://ap2-protocol.org/); [Stripe](https://docs.stripe.com/agentic-commerce/concepts/shared-payment-tokens)
- Deterministic, out-of-band enforcement is hypothesized (not established) to be a harder target for adaptive attackers than in-band defenses; it costs utility and compute (about 15x LLM calls in one reproduction). (fetched) — [arXiv:2606.26479](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.26479v1)

### Inferences
- Likely trajectory (my synthesis): near term (shipping now) — confinement plus confirmation: app allowlists, isolated task windows, mandatory confirmation for purchases, visible indicators, activity logs, hardened models. Medium term — semantic action layers (app functions, accessibility-node-level actions with package provenance) that let reference monitors, taint labels and risk-adaptive, action-bound trusted confirmation work; identity/mandate protocols take over the authorization and payment steps. Long term/research — plan-before-observe architectures and IFC for GUI, on-device perception with cloud planning, tamper-evident logs tied to signed intent.
- The economic logic behind "GUI to structured": every structural defense in section 1 and every delegation mechanism in section 4 needs typed actions and attributable principals. GUI operation persists because app/site adoption of structured interfaces is slow and some providers will refuse to expose them; so the long tail of GUI operation is exactly where the weakest guarantees and the least cooperative counterparties coincide.
- Unsolved problems, in rough order of importance for screen agents:
  1. Data-flow (not just control-flow) integrity when the observation is pixels: branch steering, spoofed UI, and legitimate-looking but attacker-authored content within an allowed app.
  2. Provenance at the level of screen regions exposed to the model as an enforceable trust label (especially webviews and user-generated content inside trusted apps).
  3. Utility cost: single-shot planning loses a large fraction of capability; trust-based memory defenses erase memory's value.
  4. Confirmation that stays meaningful: binding, anti-flooding, risk calibration, without training users to tap "Allow".
  5. Identity and delegation for agents that act through the user's own logged-in session (ambient authority), including how on-device agents get attestable identities without harming privacy.
  6. Irreversible actions and liability: who bears loss when an injected agent pays or sends; which evidence counts.
  7. Evaluation: no standard adaptive-attack benchmark for mobile agents; vendor numbers are against self-chosen attacks.
  8. Third-party agents built on the Accessibility API, which get near-total device authority with none of the first-party confinement.
  9. Supply chain of non-code artifacts (skills, app guidance cards, tool descriptions, memories).

### Gaps
- Direct 2026 statements from Schneier, Carlini, Tramèr or Shumailov on "GUI vs structured interfaces" as a prediction were not retrieved; the direction above is inferred from their papers and from platform roadmaps, not from quoted forecasts.
- No quantitative data on what share of agent actions on Android go through AppFunctions vs UI automation.
- Apple's direction (App Intents-based agents vs screen operation) was not researched in this session.
- Regulatory drivers (EU AI Act, payment regulation on agent-initiated transactions) are outside this note's scope and were not researched.
